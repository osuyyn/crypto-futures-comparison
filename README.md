## What a crypto futures trading platform actually does

A crypto futures platform gives you access to derivative contracts whose value tracks an underlying cryptocurrency. You generally do not need to buy and hold the actual coin to take a directional position.

A futures trader can:

- Open a long position when expecting the contract price to rise.
- Open a short position when expecting the contract price to fall.
- Use margin instead of paying the full notional value of the position.
- Apply leverage to increase exposure.
- Use stop-loss and take-profit orders.
- Hedge an existing spot position.
- Trade perpetual contracts without a fixed expiry date.
- Trade expiry futures that settle at a defined time.

Perpetual futures are especially common in crypto markets because they do not have a normal expiration date. Instead, funding payments help keep the contract price aligned with the spot market. That funding payment is separate from the trading commission, so a position can have a low entry fee and still become expensive to hold if funding moves against it.

OKX explains that futures costs can include the opening and closing trading fees, funding fees for perpetual contracts, liquidation-related charges, and settlement fees for expiry products.

The practical takeaway is simple: a “low-fee” platform should be judged by the complete cost of a trade, not by one number on a comparison page.

## What to compare before choosing a platform

Search results for crypto futures platforms tend to focus on the same handful of questions: fees, leverage, liquidity, supported markets, demo trading, risk controls, and regional access. Those are the right categories, but they need to be examined in the correct order.

### 1. Fee structure

Most crypto futures platforms use maker and taker fees.

A **maker order** adds liquidity to the order book. It usually rests on the book before being filled. A **taker order** executes immediately against existing liquidity and normally carries the higher fee.

The distinction depends on how the order is filled, not simply whether you selected a limit order. A limit order can still be treated as a taker order if it executes immediately. OKX specifically notes that the final fee depends on the actual execution, including whether an order is filled as maker or taker and whether it is filled in multiple parts.

For a basic example, suppose you open and close a position with a total notional value of $10,000:

- At a 0.02% maker fee, one side costs about $2.
- At a 0.05% taker fee, one side costs about $5.
- A maker-to-maker round trip would cost about $4 before funding, spread, and slippage.
- A taker-to-taker round trip would cost about $10 before funding, spread, and slippage.

Those numbers are only illustrations. The actual rate depends on your account, product group, trading volume, VIP level, and region. OKX instructs users to check the fee displayed in the order panel for the specific futures pair and account.

### 2. Funding rates

Funding is not the same as a trading fee.

Perpetual futures use funding payments to help keep the perpetual contract close to the spot price. Depending on the funding rate and whether you are long or short, you may pay funding or receive it. The rate can change over time, so a strategy that holds positions for several days should monitor funding rather than only looking at entry and exit fees.

For short-term traders, maker and taker fees may matter most. For swing traders, funding can become a larger part of the total cost. For high-leverage traders, both can be significant because commissions are calculated against the position’s notional value rather than only the margin deposited.

### 3. Liquidation rules and margin mode

Leverage does not make a trade safer. It reduces the amount of capital required to control a position, which also means that a smaller adverse price movement can consume your margin.

Platforms commonly offer:

- **Isolated margin**, where a specific amount of margin is assigned to one position.
- **Cross margin**, where available collateral may be shared across positions.
- Position-level leverage settings.
- Maintenance-margin requirements.
- Liquidation-price estimates.
- Stop-loss and take-profit orders.
- Risk-limit or position-tier rules.

OKX’s risk disclosures explain that margin mode affects how collateral is applied and the circumstances in which liquidation may occur. Isolated margin ring-fences the margin allocated to an individual position, while other account configurations can expose a broader collateral balance to a position’s losses.

A sensible workflow is to calculate the liquidation price before placing the order, then decide whether the position size remains acceptable. If the trade only works when using very high leverage, the position is probably too large for the account.

### 4. Available markets

The right platform needs to list the contracts you actually intend to trade.

Common categories include:

- USDT-margined perpetuals.
- USDC-margined perpetuals.
- Coin-margined perpetuals.
- Expiry futures.
- Pre-market futures.
- Index or thematic contracts where available.

OKX’s learning materials describe futures markets covering major assets such as BTC, ETH, SOL, ADA, and XRP, along with other listed assets. The exact contract list changes, and product availability can differ by region and account type, so the trading interface remains the final source for currently available pairs.

More markets are not automatically better. A long list of thinly traded contracts can bring wider spreads and more slippage. For most traders, a smaller selection of liquid markets is easier to manage than dozens of contracts with inconsistent execution.
